Abstract

Active Learning (Angluin, 1988, Cohn et al., 1994) is a paradigm where a concept is learnt by an
intelligent machine through the interaction of an oracle with the machine system, over a sequence of
learning epochs. A distinctive component in an active system is the querying strategy, used to sample
candidate training instances from a pool and evaluate them for informativeness, i.e., their potential
to improve the classification model. Various strategies have been proposed including; Query by
Committee (Seung et al., 1992), Monte Carlo estimation (Roy and McCallum, 2001), uncertainty
sampling (Lewis and Gale, 1994) and version space reduction (margin sampling) (Tong and Koller,
2001). Irrespective of how the most informative pattern instances are chosen at each epoch they are
presented for labeling, to a machine (database) or human oracle that has knowledge of the class
labels. After adding the newly labeled instances to the current training set, a revised model is
subsequently induced. A goal of this process is to achieve an efficient, incremental advancement of
learning at each epoch. The process is continued until some stopping criterion has been met, such as
reaching a stage where information from the selected instances is contrary to the model, causing the
classifier confidence to drop, as in Vlachos (2008)

Introduction

For centuries, humans have talked about living on other planets.The earliest record dates to
the 17th century, when John Wilkins (1640), a natural philosopher and former Bishop, proposed
that ambitious adventurers such as Columbus might one day reach the Moon and make it habitable. 
Because of the lack of knowledge and resources at the time, no one attemptedto live on other
planets. However, later in the 20th century, with the rise of spaceflight and the moon landing,
colonisation no longer seemed impossible, and it became a real engineering question. 

Mars was one of the earliest celestial objects tracked in writing (by Babylonian astronomers 
in the 2nd millennium BCE). It has been called many names since,mainly because of its colour,
e.g., "Nergal" by the Babylonians, "Ares" by theGreeks, and "Mangala" by Hindu astronomers. 
It was always seen as a harsh anddangerous place (Byrne, 2026). 

Fast forward to the 20th century, more detailed images of Mars' surface revealedOlympus Mons 
(the largest volcano in the solar system), ancient river channels, and a vast canyon system, 
Valles Marineris. These landscapes are like Earth's. This suggests that the Martian environment
may be suitable to start a new era ofmankind in space. 

This article explores the many hurdles we must clear to face this alien environment, as well
as projects that have happened and are happening to do with it.

Atmosphere: 


Mars is much colder than Earth, with temperatures ranging from 20 degrees Celsius to –153 
degrees Celsius. Due to its thin atmosphere, heat from the Sun easily escapes. A potential
method of heating this planet is through the volcanoes on it. 


The Volcanoes: 


Mars's volcanoes are concentrated in the Tharsis and Elysium provinces, near the equator, and are
much larger than those on Earth. Olympus Mons, for example, is more than 132,000 feet tall, and
its base covers an area as large as the state of Arizona. Hot gas from these volcanoes could
help to build a warmer climate for humans and plants. The heat can also be converted into geothermal
energy. Mars had an active volcanic past; however, currently there is little to no volcanic activity.
Scientists have come up with different ways to heat up the atmosphere, such as nuking (Musk 2015) or
redirecting asteroids to hit the surface of Mars. This however, raises many legal and scientific
questions. 


Cyanobacteria: 


Microorganisms called cyanobacteria could be introduced to the Martian environment, which converted 
Earth's atmosphere through photosynthesis billions of years ago (Mehta, 2021). This process, however,
took many years to fully convert Earth's atmosphere, and it will be so for Mars. 

 

Its atmosphere consists mostly of carbon dioxide, nitrogen and argon, all of which are not breathable for 
humans. In this instance. A biome could be made with trees and plants that perform photosynthesis and 
produce oxygen. A plantation would also mean crops are grown on Mars as a source of food and perhaps
habitation for animals. 

 


Water: 

As mentioned in the introduction, the Martian surface has old river channels, suggesting it once had 
water; however, because its atmosphere is too thin, liquid water could not exist. Figure 1 is a recent
picture from Mars showing narrow lines on slopes that suggest water could still run down them today; 
this water can now be seen as ice under the surface in the polar regions. 

Figure 1: 




Some of this water can also be seen in the form of hydrated minerals. Water can be extracted from this 
ice and minerals by Martian astronauts by heating them. This water can also be broken down into its 
fundamental parts : Hydrogen and oxygen. Hydrogen can be used in making fuel for rockets, and oxygen 
can be used for breathing.
